The Conductor's Digital Companion

A one-thumb, four-second, full-sun ticketing app for BMTC & BEST conductors — replacing the worn thermal ETM with a phone that keeps up with the bus.

01

The machine is fighting the conductor

Indian city buses run on the Electronic Ticketing Machine — a belt-hung thermal-printer brick that has barely changed in fifteen years. Watch any boarding and three things happen at once:

  • The hardware is worn out. Keypads are pressed thousands of times a shift; legends fade, buttons stick, and the low-contrast LCD washes out completely past 30° of sun.
  • Boarding is a burst, not a stream. Six to ten passengers swarm in twenty seconds, each with a different stop, while the driver holds the bus and traffic builds behind it.
  • Cash is the real interface. The conductor's hands hold notes and coins; change is computed under pressure. The screen gets whatever attention is left over — which is almost none.

This synthesis is built without fieldwork: it draws on the stated constraints, public documentation of BMTC/BEST ETM operations, and everyday commute observation. Everything below is decision → rationale, with the assumptions registered at the end.

The design target, precisely

2 taps + 1 confirm

to select a destination and issue for 3 passengers — measured at ≈2.2s in the prototype against a 4.0s budget, leaving half the budget for physical cash exchange.

02

Three constraints, three design principles

Every constraint converts directly into a rule the interface must obey. These three rules govern every screen that follows.

One hand · jerky bus

The One-Thumb Contract

100% of per-transaction actions live inside the natural thumb arc — the bottom ~55% of the screen. The interaction grammar is tap-only: drags, pinches and long-presses lose to the road. Every mistake is reversible within a 3-second window.

< 4 seconds

Defaults do the work

GPS + route position predict the next stop and pre-load its fares, so destination selection is usually one tap on an already-ranked chip. The count pad is direct-set — tapping 3 is the entry, no plus-plus-plus. Printing runs in the background so the phone never blocks on hardware.

Glare · micro-glances

One glance, one answer

AAA contrast (≥7:1) on a near-black base; money is always in safety-yellow. Hero numerals at 56–64px, weight ≥600, tabular figures so digits never shift. Each screen answers exactly one question — the fare, the change, or the count — never all three competing.

03

The flow, live

This is a working prototype, not a mockup. Run the scripted 4-second test, or tap through it yourself exactly as a conductor would — every control is thumb-placed. Jump to the phone ↓

0.0–0.6s

Standby already knows. Raise-to-wake + GPS pin the stop. The screen shows TRINITY CIRCLE — next stop with fares pre-loaded and predicted chips ranked.

0.6–1.1s

Tap the destination chip. One tap confirms what the passengers just said. If they name something unusual, the full stop list is one more tap away.

1.1–1.6s

Tap “3”. Direct-set count pad: one tap is the whole entry. Concessions are a pre-sale toggle, not a per-passenger mode.

1.6–2.1s

Tap ISSUE. Green flash, double haptic, chime — confirmed by ear before the eyes check. Printing and change-counting now happen in parallel; the phone never waits.

04

Navigation & reachability strategy

Two ideas organize the whole app: a flat three-state loop for navigation, and the One-Thumb Contract for placement. Together they mean a conductor can run an entire shift without ever hunting for a screen.

  1. Frequency maps to height. The more often an action happens per sale, the lower it sits. ISSUE and the count pad live in the effortless arc; the shift log lives in the reach-hurt corner — deliberately, because reaching there must never interrupt a sale.
  2. Tap-only grammar. No drags, no pinch-zoom, no press-and-hold required. A control that needs a steady hand is a control that fails on a speed breaker. Hold-to-repeat exists only as an accelerator, never as the only path.
  3. Primary action = bottom, big, dominant hand. ISSUE is a 96px-tall target in the bottom corner of the thumb arc — the easiest place to hit without looking. Left-hand mode mirrors the entire layout.
  4. Hardware does double duty. Volume keys act as +/− passenger count in the sell state — muscle memory carried over from physical ETM keys (simulated with +/− keys in the web prototype; captured natively on Android).
  5. Every error is a 3-second regret, not a 3-minute fix. Undo toast after every issue; direct-set count pad means a mis-tap is corrected by simply tapping the right number; void-from-log handles anything older.
05

Built for full sun and half-second glances

Glare is an information problem, not a brightness problem: washed-out screens fail because their signal-to-noise collapses. The counter-strategy is to raise contrast, grow the signal, and delete the noise.

06

Feedback you can trust by ear

A conductor's eyes belong to passengers and traffic, not the screen. So confirmations ride on sound and haptics first — the display is the backup, not the channel.

Any tapControl presses down (scale 0.97)—8ms tick

Rejected / mis-tapControl shakes, nothing changesLow 200 Hz buzzLong buzz
Offline queueBadge + running count, sale continuesSilent by designNone — never interrupts a sale

Tones are tuned to cut 85 dB of diesel and traffic; the pattern language is three-meanings-only (done / money / wrong) so it never needs interpretation. Sound and haptics are simulated in the web prototype — on device, both are native.

07

Edge cases are the product

Conductors work at the edge of the network, the battery and the law of large numbers. Every state below is implemented in the prototype unless marked otherwise.

Dead zone offline

Sales never stop. Tickets queue on-device with a visible count and sync when signal returns; nothing blocks, nothing beeps.

GPS pins the wrong stop

One tap on the “wrong stop” chip opens the ranked stop list; the correction also re-trains that route's prediction weights.

Printer dies mid-shift

The ETM's Bluetooth printer is reused, not required. Failover: digital ticket with QR for the passenger to show on inspection. (Simulated via the Printer off toggle.)

Wrong quantity, just issued

3-second undo toast on every issue, then void-from-log for anything older. Direct-set count pad also makes the next sale self-correcting.

Passenger pays by UPI

One tap switches settle to a full-screen dynamic QR; verification confirms with the same chime as cash. Change-calc never appears for UPI.

Short change

Conductor marks “short ₹X given” in one tap — logged for the depot report instead of silently vanishing into today's tally.

Route diversion

Manual stage mode: conductor picks the fare band directly (≤2 km / 3–5 km …), decoupled from the broken GPS trail.

More than 9 passengers

Direct-set pad caps at 9; the app prompts a split sale — which mirrors how bundles of tickets are physically handed over anyway.

08

Assumptions & what I'd test first

Stated assumptions

  • The conductor holds the phone in the dominant hand with a wrist/shoulder strap; the other hand manages cash. Left-hand mode covers the reverse.
  • The existing Bluetooth thermal printer is reused as a companion; the phone is a standard Android device (AMOLED assumed for the battery claim).
  • Fares, stages and route 335E are illustrative, modeled on BMTC stage-based pricing; real fare tables would be config, not design.
  • Synthesis is desk research + commute observation; no conductor interviews were possible inside this exercise window.

First week with real conductors, I'd test

  1. Ride-alongs on 3 routes × 5 conductors, measuring actual tap-to-tap times against my 2.2s claim — the prediction ranking is the biggest unknown.
  2. Whether predicted destination chips match how conductors think (destinations vs. stage counts) — this decides the whole sell screen.
  3. Chime legibility at real cabin noise, and whether the 3s undo window matches real error-notice latency.
  4. Receipt policy with conductors and checkers: is a QR digital ticket acceptable evidence, or does paper remain mandatory on some services?

The one-sentence thesis

Speed and safety come from defaults, not from faster fingers.

Prediction, pre-loading and parallel hardware shrink the interaction to two taps — so the conductor's scarce attention can go back to the road, the cash and the passengers.
